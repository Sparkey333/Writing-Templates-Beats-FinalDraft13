# Sanderson's Laws of Magic

> These aren't rules everyone must follow — they're observations about what makes *my* magic
> systems work. — **Brandon Sanderson**, from his essays and
> [BYU lectures](../08-resources/brandon-sanderson-resources.md).

Three laws (plus a "Zeroth") for designing magic that feels satisfying instead of arbitrary.
Read Sanderson's own essays — they're short and excellent:
[First](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/sandersons-first-law) ·
[Second](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/sandersons-second-law) ·
[Third](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/sandersons-third-law-of-magic).

## Zeroth Law
> **Always err on the side of what's awesome.** Rules serve the story and the reader's
> experience, not the other way around. If a law below fights your story's coolest moment,
> the story wins.

## First Law
> **An author's ability to solve conflict with magic is directly proportional to how well the
> reader understands that magic.**

- **Hard magic** (reader understands the rules → magic *can* solve problems). If Sanderson's
  Allomancy lets you "Push" on metals with known limits, a clever metal‑Push climax feels
  *earned*, because the reader could see it coming.
- **Soft magic** (reader doesn't understand the rules → magic should *not* resolve the climax;
  it creates wonder and problems instead). Gandalf's power is vague — so Gandalf can't just
  magic the Ring into Mordor; that would feel like cheating.
- **The failure mode it prevents:** *deus ex machina* — a new, unexplained power that
  conveniently saves the day. If your hero wins with a capability the reader didn't know
  existed, you've broken the First Law.

## Second Law
> **Limitations > Powers.** What a magic *can't* do is more interesting than what it can.

- Weaknesses, costs, and consequences generate drama; raw power doesn't. Superman is
  interesting because of kryptonite and his moral code, not because he's strong.
- Design **costs** (what does using it drain/risk?), **limitations** (hard boundaries), and
  **weaknesses** (exploitable flaws). The tighter the box, the more clever the escapes.

## Third Law
> **Expand what you already have before you add something new.** Go deeper, not wider.

- Before inventing a second magic system, wring more consequences out of the first: how does
  it reshape the **economy, warfare, religion, politics, class, daily life**?
- Interconnection makes a world feel real. One magic system with ten thoughtful implications
  beats five shallow ones.

## Design checklist
```text
MAGIC SYSTEM: ____________________
[ ] Hard or soft? (how much does the reader understand?)   → sets what it can resolve
[ ] What can it DO (powers)?
[ ] What can it NOT do (limitations)?  ← spend the most time here (2nd Law)
[ ] What does it COST (price/risk)?
[ ] What's its WEAKNESS (exploit)?
[ ] Who has access / who doesn't? (social consequence)
[ ] 3rd-Law ripple: economy? war? religion? politics? tech? art?
[ ] 1st-Law audit: does the climax use only rules the reader already knew?
[ ] Zeroth check: is it awesome?
```
