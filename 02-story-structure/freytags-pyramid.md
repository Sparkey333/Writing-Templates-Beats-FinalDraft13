# Freytag's Pyramid — 5 Parts

> Exposition → Rising Action → Climax → Falling Action → Catastrophe.
> — **Gustav Freytag**, *Die Technik des Dramas* (*Technique of the Drama*, 1863)

Freytag analyzed five‑act classical and Shakespearean tragedy and drew it as a **pyramid**:
tension rises to a peak, then falls. It's the oldest formal model here and the best lens for
**tragedy** and classical drama.

## When to use it
- Analyzing or writing tragedy / classical five‑act drama.
- As a teaching lens — it's where "rising action / climax / falling action" comes from.
- Note: modern commercial stories usually put the climax near the *end* (75–95%), not the
  literal middle; Freytag's symmetric pyramid is a tragedy model, not a modern default.

## The 5 parts
1. **Exposition** — establish characters, setting, situation; ends with an *exciting force*
   (*erregendes Moment*) that starts the conflict.
2. **Rising Action** — complications and mounting tension toward the peak.
3. **Climax** — the turning point at the apex; the protagonist's fortunes peak or pivot.
4. **Falling Action** — consequences unwind; a "moment of final suspense" may tease reversal.
5. **Dénouement / Resolution — or Catastrophe** — the end. In tragedy this is the
   **Catastrophe** (the hero's downfall); in comedy/modern usage, the resolution (order
   restored).

```
              3. CLIMAX
            /            \
   2. RISING            4. FALLING
      ACTION               ACTION
     /                          \
 1. EXPOSITION            5. CATASTROPHE /
 (exciting force)            RESOLUTION
```

## Worked example — *Macbeth*
Exposition: the witches' prophecy. Rising Action: Macbeth murders Duncan, seizes the throne,
kills to hold it. Climax (Act 3): Banquo's murder / the banquet ghost — Macbeth's fortunes
peak and begin to crack. Falling Action: Macduff rallies, Lady Macbeth unravels. Catastrophe:
"Birnam Wood," Macbeth slain — the tragic downfall.

