# Kishōtenketsu — The 4‑Act Structure (Conflict Optional)

> 起承転結 — *ki* (introduction), *shō* (development), *ten* (twist), *ketsu* (conclusion).
> A classical East Asian narrative structure (Chinese *qǐ‑chéng‑zhuǎn‑hé*), used across
> Japanese, Chinese, and Korean poetry and story.

The most useful *non‑Western* framework to know — because it can build a satisfying story
**without conflict or an antagonist**. The engine isn't a fight; it's a **reframe**. Act 3
introduces something seemingly unrelated, and Act 4 reveals the connection, recontextualizing
everything.

## When to use it
- Slice‑of‑life, literary, mood/atmosphere, children's stories, contemplative games, comics
  (it's the backbone of many 4‑panel manga).
- When a forced conflict would cheapen the piece.
- Twist and mystery reveals (the *ten* is a natural "turn").

## The 4 acts
| Act | Name | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Ki (起) — Introduction** | Establish characters, place, situation. No conflict needed. |
| 2 | **Shō (承) — Development** | Deepen/continue it. Still calm; let the reader settle in. |
| 3 | **Ten (転) — Twist / Turn** | Introduce something new, unexpected, or seemingly unrelated — the pivot. |
| 4 | **Ketsu (結) — Conclusion** | Reconcile: reveal how the twist connects; the whole reframes. |

The magic is in the **relationship between Ten and the first two acts** — the twist makes you
re‑read acts 1–2 in a new light.

## Worked example — a classic 4‑panel structure
Ki: A girl waters a plant on her windowsill. Shō: Day by day she tends it; it grows.
Ten: A boy in the opposite window has been growing the *same* plant. Ketsu: They wave — the
two windows, and two lonelinesses, were connected all along. No villain; complete story.

## Western vs. Kishōtenketsu
- **Western (3‑act):** desire → obstacle → conflict → resolution. Momentum from *tension*.
- **Kishōtenketsu:** setup → development → **surprising reframe** → synthesis. Momentum from
  *curiosity and recontextualization*.
- You can hybridize: use a Ten‑style reframe as your Midpoint or twist inside a 3‑act plot.

## Blank template
```text
TITLE:
1. KI (introduction) —
2. SHŌ (development) —
3. TEN (the twist / new element) —
4. KETSU (how it all connects; the reframe) —

CHECK: Does the Ketsu make the reader re-see Ki + Shō? Is the Ten a genuine surprise
that's fair in hindsight (not random)?
```
