# Horror — The Dread Curve

> Escalating wrongness, engineered. Genre‑pattern analysis of horror structure across film
> and fiction (slasher, cosmic, haunted‑house, and body‑horror traditions).

## The promise
Sustained dread that pays off in a release (a scream, a survival, or a final gut‑punch) —
and, often, a thematic confrontation with something the story has been *about* all along
(grief, guilt, a specific fear made literal).

## The shape (a specialized [Fichtean Curve](../02-story-structure/fichtean-curve.md))
Horror rarely uses a single climax — it's a **staircase of escalating "wrongness,"** each
step past the point of easy retreat.

1. **The normal, slightly off** — establish safety with one wrong detail the audience
   notices before the characters do (dramatic irony as dread engine).
2. **First transgression / discovery** — someone crosses a line (enters the house, reads the
   book, wakes the thing) — often small, deniable.
3. **Escalating incidents, each explainable... barely** — 2–3 events the characters
   rationalize away; the audience can't.
4. **The point of no return** — undeniable confirmation of the threat; retreat is no longer
   simple (isolation is often engineered here — dead phones, blocked roads, closing doors).
5. **Rules of the threat established** — what can stop it, what it wants, its weakness (or
   the horror of *no* weakness) — see
   [Sanderson's First Law](../04-worldbuilding/sanderson-laws-of-magic.md) applied to
   monsters: a survivable ending needs the rules shown before they're used.
6. **Losses mount** — the cast thins; each loss raises the read‑cost of the next risk.
7. **The confrontation** — direct engagement with the threat, using the established rules
   (or the horror of the rules failing).
8. **The scream / climax** — maximum intensity; the dread curve peaks.
9. **The final image / "final girl" or the sting** — survival that's scarred, ambiguous, or
   undercut by a last-second sting implying it isn't over.

## Craft notes
- **Show less, imply more — until you don't.** Dread lives in the gap between what's implied
  and what's confirmed; horror's biggest craft risk is revealing the monster too early/fully
  and converting dread into simple action‑movie threat.
- **Give the threat internal logic** (even cosmic horror has rules — "don't look," "don't say
  the name," "it can't cross running water"). Rule‑less threats can't build fair tension;
  the audience needs *something* to hope characters do right.
- **The monster is usually a metaphor** — grief, addiction, guilt, a specific societal fear.
  Know what yours means; it sharpens every beat above.

## Variations & subversions
- **The dread never resolves** (ambiguous/cosmic horror) — subverts beat 8–9's release
  entirely; the horror is that nothing can meaningfully "win."
- **Comedy‑horror** — undercut dread rhythmically with relief beats; requires *more*
  structural discipline, not less, so tonal whiplash reads as intentional.
- **Slow‑burn literary horror** — stretch beats 1–3 across most of the book; the "point of no
  return" arrives very late.

## Blank template
```text
THE THREAT (and what it's a metaphor for):
NORMAL, SLIGHTLY OFF (the one wrong detail) —
FIRST TRANSGRESSION —
ESCALATING INCIDENTS (2-3, explainable... barely) —
POINT OF NO RETURN (how retreat is closed off) —
RULES OF THE THREAT (what stops it / its weakness, if any) —
LOSSES (who, and in what order) —
CONFRONTATION —
CLIMAX (the scream) —
FINAL IMAGE / STING —
```
