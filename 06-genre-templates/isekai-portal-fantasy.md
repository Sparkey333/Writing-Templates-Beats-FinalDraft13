# Isekai / Portal Fantasy Template

> "Isekai" (異世界, "different world") — a Japanese genre label for portal‑fantasy/
> transported‑protagonist stories; the Western tradition (*Narnia*, *Alice in Wonderland*,
> *The Wizard of Oz*, *A Connecticut Yankee in King Arthur's Court*) runs parallel and older.

## The promise
An ordinary (often powerless or overlooked) person is pulled into an extraordinary world
where the normal rules of their old life no longer apply — and where they can become
someone new.

## Obligatory beats
1. **Ordinary/mundane life established** — usually brief; the point is the *contrast*
   with what's coming, and often a lack/dissatisfaction (see
   [Story Circle](../02-story-structure/story-circle.md) beat 1–2).
2. **The crossing** — death/accident/summoning/portal pulls them out of their world. In
   modern isekai this is frequently **literal death** (reincarnation isekai) or a summoning
   ritual; in Western portal fantasy, more often a literal door/wardrobe/storm.
3. **Rules of the new world explained** — often via a system (game‑like stats, magic
   classes, a guide/mentor NPC) — see
   [Sanderson's First Law](../04-worldbuilding/sanderson-laws-of-magic.md): the faster and
   clearer the rules land, the sooner the world can be used to solve problems satisfyingly.
4. **Unexpected advantage** — the protagonist has a skill/knowledge from their old world
   that's disproportionately powerful here (modern knowledge, a game‑cheat stat, plain
   decency in a cynical world).
5. **Found family / new community** — replaces or repairs what was missing at home.
6. **A local‑stakes arc** — the new world's own conflict, which the protagonist is drawn
   into (often layered with the [power‑up serial](./toonami-shonen-powerup-arc.md) template).
7. **The choice to stay (or the impossibility of return)** — thematically the crux: was the
   old world ever really home?
8. **Resolution** — full integration into the new world, OR a bittersweet return that proves
   they were changed regardless.

## Craft notes
- **The "unexpected advantage" beat is the genre's core wish‑fulfillment engine** — be
  deliberate about what it says thematically (is competence rewarded? kindness? geek
  knowledge? this is often the whole appeal).
- **Exposition‑dump risk is highest here** — a new world needs its rules taught, but
  isekai's worst habit is stopping the story to lecture. Teach rules through use (see
  Inception's Ariadne‑as‑audience‑surrogate technique in the
  [Inception beat sheet](../01-screenplay/beat-sheets/inception-beat-sheet.md)).
- **"Why can't they just go home?"** needs a real answer early, or readers will keep asking.

## Variations & subversions
- **They CAN go home, easily — and choose not to** (subverts the "trapped" default,
  raises the emotional stakes of the choice itself).
- **The new world is worse, not better** — subverts wish‑fulfillment for a survival/horror
  tone.
- **They remember nothing of their old life** — merges with amnesia‑plot mechanics.
- **Multiple isekai'd protagonists meet as rivals** — turns the "unexpected advantage" beat
  into a competition.

## Blank template
```text
OLD WORLD — what's lacking there:
THE CROSSING — how/why (death / summon / portal / other):
NEW WORLD RULES — taught through: ___ (use, not lecture)
UNEXPECTED ADVANTAGE — what carries over, and WHY it matters thematically:
FOUND FAMILY —
LOCAL-STAKES ARC (may layer with power-up-serial template):
CAN THEY RETURN? — why/why not (answer this EARLY):
ENDING — stay [ ] / return changed [ ] / other:
```

## Exemplars
Western portal fantasy: *The Chronicles of Narnia* (C.S. Lewis), *Alice's Adventures in
Wonderland* (Lewis Carroll). Isekai: a large modern anime/light‑novel subgenre — consult
official releases for specific titles.
