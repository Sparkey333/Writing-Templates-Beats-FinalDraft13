# The Snowflake Method

> Good fiction doesn't get written by accident. Design first, then write.
> — **Randy Ingermanson** ("the Snowflake Guy"), *How to Write a Novel Using the Snowflake
> Method*. Original article: https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/articles/snowflake-method/

The Snowflake Method grows a novel the way a fractal snowflake grows — from a tiny, simple
core out to full complexity, one **doubling** at a time. You never face the blank page; you
only ever *expand what you already have.* This is the flagship planning method in this repo,
and the [FD13 × Snowflake master template](../01-screenplay/templates/final-draft-snowflake-master-template.md)
and the [app's planner](../app/README.md) are built directly on it.

## Why it works
- Each step is a small, non‑threatening expansion of the previous one.
- You find structural problems while they're cheap to fix (in a sentence), not after 40,000
  words.
- It naturally surfaces **decisions you haven't made yet** — the "unsure" items this repo's
  app collects into a focus list.

## The 10 steps
| # | Step | Output | ~Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **One‑sentence summary** | A 15‑word logline of the whole novel (no character names — roles). | 1 hr |
| 2 | **One‑paragraph summary** | Expand step 1 to ~5 sentences: setup, 3 disasters, ending. (The 3 disasters ≈ the three act turns.) | 1 hr |
| 3 | **Character summary sheets** | For each major character: name, one‑line arc, motivation (abstract want), goal (concrete want), conflict, epiphany, and their own one‑paragraph storyline. | — |
| 4 | **One‑page synopsis** | Expand each sentence of step 2 into a full paragraph → ~1 page. Each paragraph ends in a disaster (except the last = resolution). | — |
| 5 | **Character synopses** | A half‑to‑full page per major character telling the story *from their POV*. | — |
| 6 | **Four‑page synopsis** | Expand each paragraph of step 4 into a page → ~4 pages of full narrative logic. | — |
| 7 | **Character bibles** | Full dossiers per character (birthdate, description, history, motivations, how they change). See [character dossier](../05-character/character-dossier-template.md). | — |
| 8 | **Scene list** | A spreadsheet: one row per scene — POV, what happens, and the value it turns. | — |
| 9 | **Scene narrative (optional)** | A paragraph or "scene brief" per scene: the conflict, the setback, and the POV goal. | — |
| 10 | **Write the first draft** | You now have everything; drafting becomes expansion, not invention. | — |

> Ingermanson's rule: **if a step isn't helping you, skip it.** The method is a scaffold, not
> a cage. Most writers find steps 1, 2, 4, and 8 indispensable.

## The three‑disaster spine (steps 2 & 4)
Ingermanson frames the one‑paragraph summary as five sentences:
1. **Setup** — the backdrop and the story problem.
2. **Disaster 1** — end of Act 1 (~25%): forces the hero into the main conflict.
3. **Disaster 2** — Midpoint (~50%): the plan fails / everything changes.
4. **Disaster 3** — end of Act 2 (~75%): the lowest point, sets up the finale.
5. **Ending** — the resolution.

Notice this maps cleanly onto [Three‑Act](../02-story-structure/three-act-structure.md) and
[Save the Cat](../02-story-structure/save-the-cat-15-beats.md) — Snowflake is the *process*,
those are the *shape*.

## For series & screen
- **Series:** run steps 1–2 for the *series* arc, then steps 1–8 per book/season. Keep a
  master "series bible" (characters + world persist; see [worldbuilding](../04-worldbuilding/)).
- **Screenplay:** the Snowflake maps onto a screenplay just as well — see the
  [FD13 × Snowflake master template](../01-screenplay/templates/final-draft-snowflake-master-template.md),
  which pairs each Snowflake step with the corresponding Final Draft element.
